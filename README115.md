**(1) Peptide Bonds in p53**
* **Structure:** All 393 amino acids in human wild-type p53 are connected by covalent peptide bonds, extending from the N-terminal to the C-terminal domain.
* **Cancer Link:** Missense mutations alter specific amino acids, disrupting the peptide backbone's geometry and causing the core DNA-binding domain (residues 102–292) to misfold.

**(2) Hydrogen Bonding in p53**
* **A. Secondary Structure & Stability**
  * **Backbone H-Bonds:** Stabilize $\beta$-sheets and loops in the DNA-binding domain.
  * **Tetramerization:** H-bonds in $\alpha$-helices (residues 307–355) allow four p53 subunits to assemble into a functional tetramer.
* **B. DNA Binding & Hotspot Mutations**
  * **Base & Backbone Contacts:** Polar/charged side chains (Arg, Lys, Asn, Gln) form crucial hydrogen bonds directly with target DNA sequences.
  * **Mutational Impact (e.g., Arg273):** Position Arg273 normally forms essential H-bonds with the DNA backbone. Mutating Arg273 destroys these bonds, preventing DNA binding and leading to tumor growth.

**Question:** How do nucleotides assemble to form double-stranded DNA? What are the basic structural components of DNA? Is DNA right-handed or left-handed? List the names of the four DNA bases. Which bases form hydrogen bonds with each other?

**Answer:**

![DNA Structure](image/Profile.png)

* **Basic Components:** Each nucleotide contains a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base.
* **Assembly:** Single strands form via covalent phosphodiester bonds. Two strands twist into an antiparallel double helix stabilized by hydrogen bonds and base stacking.
* **Handedness:** Standard biological DNA (B-DNA) is right-handed.
* **Four Bases:** Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Cytosine (C), and Guanine (G).
* **Base Pairing:** A pairs with T (2 H-bonds); C pairs with G (3 H-bonds).

### 4. 【Return to the Crime Scene】
**Question:** Why can a mutation (change) in just one amino acid of a protein sometimes lead to a serious human disease? Give one specific example of such a disease.

**Answer:**
A single amino acid change can drastically alter a protein's 3D structure and chemical properties, often rendering it dysfunctional. Because a protein's function depends entirely on its precise shape and ability to interact with other molecules (like DNA or other proteins), swapping even one building block can disrupt folding, stability, or active sites, leading to disease.

* **Specific Example: Sickle Cell Anemia**
  * **The Mutation:** In the gene encoding hemoglobin (the protein that carries oxygen in red blood cells), a single nucleotide change replaces the codon for Glutamic acid with the codon for Valine at position 6 of the $\beta$-globin chain.
  * **The Chemical Impact:** Glutamic acid is hydrophilic (water-loving), while Valine is hydrophobic (water-fearing).
  * **The Resulting Disease:** This single hydrophobic patch causes hemoglobin molecules to stick together and form rigid fibers when oxygen levels are low. These fibers distort red blood cells into a stiff, "sickle" shape, causing them to block blood vessels, break down prematurely, and lead to severe pain, organ damage, and anemia.
